A level philosophy and religious studies

Attributes of God

1. Can God be omniscient, omnipotent and omnibenevolent?

Classical theism and perfection

Classical theism: God is the most perfect being that could exist[cite: 1]. The three attributes are central to this conception of divine perfection[cite: 1].

Omniscient
God is all-knowing: God knows all true propositions, or all that it is logically possible to know[cite: 1].
Omnipotent
God is all-powerful: God has the power to do anything, or anything that is logically possible[cite: 1].
Omnibenevolent
God is all-good: God wills only what is morally good or always acts in the best logically possible way for creatures[cite: 1].
Omniscience — all-knowing
Aquinas
God knows all things by knowing his own essence[cite: 1]. His knowledge is perfect, unlimited and immediate: God knows things together, not successively[cite: 1].
Plantinga
Aquinas does not explain how God can know future free choices without determining them[cite: 1]. Plantinga argues that God knows what any free creature would do in any set of circumstances[cite: 1].
Process theology — Hartshorne, Whitehead and Cobb
God's knowledge is dynamic rather than complete and unchanging[cite: 1]. God knows the world as it becomes and God's knowledge grows as the world unfolds[cite: 1].
Swinburne
Omniscience means knowing all true propositions and believing no false ones[cite: 1]. Future free actions are not yet true or false, so God cannot know them in advance without undermining their freedom[cite: 1].
“To be omniscient is to know all true propositions and believe no false ones.” — Swinburne, The Coherence of Theism[cite: 1]

Biblical evidence: Psalm 139:4 says God knows words before they are spoken; Hebrews 4:13 says nothing in creation is hidden from God[cite: 1].

Omnipotence — all-powerful

Two main interpretations: God can do anything, or God can do anything that is logically possible[cite: 1].

Descartes
God could make a square circle because even necessary truths depend on God's will[cite: 1].
Aquinas
Omnipotence does not include intrinsically impossible actions or actions that would detract from God's perfection[cite: 1]. God cannot sin because sin is a defect rather than an exercise of power[cite: 1].
Antony Kenny
For God, there is no difference between having the power to do something and it being logically possible for God to do it[cite: 1]. Divine omnipotence is limited only by what is logically possible for a being with God's attributes[cite: 1].

Biblical evidence: Matthew 19:26, “With God all things are possible”; Luke 1:37, “For with God nothing is impossible.”[cite: 1]

Omnibenevolence — all-good
Anselm
God is the one supreme good, completely self-sufficient and the source on which everything else depends[cite: 1].
Aquinas
God is called good because God is the cause of goodness in things[cite: 1].
Swinburne
God is perfectly good and always acts for the best[cite: 1].

Biblical evidence: Psalm 106:1 describes God's goodness and enduring love; John 3:16 links God's love for the world with the giving of his Son; Luke 1:50 refers to God's mercy[cite: 1].

2. What is God's relationship with time?

Everlasting — God exists through time

God exists without beginning or end, within and throughout all time[cite: 1]. God exists at every moment, rather than being outside time altogether[cite: 1].

Richard Swinburne
God is everlasting in the sense of existing at each moment of time[cite: 1].
Nicholas Wolterstorff
God is everlasting rather than timeless: God exists through time[cite: 1].
Charles Hartshorne
God's existence is everlasting and temporal[cite: 1].

This model allows God to experience temporal change and makes divine knowledge of future events a question of foreknowledge[cite: 1].

Eternal — God is outside time

God is timeless and transcends the temporal order, existing without beginning or end[cite: 1]. God is outside past, present and future and surveys time in a single present[cite: 1].

Boethius
“Eternity then, is the complete possession, all at once, of illimitable life.” God experiences all temporal events in a simultaneous present[cite: 1].
Anselm
“You were not, therefore, yesterday, nor will You be tomorrow, but yesterday and today and tomorrow You are.”[cite: 1]
Aquinas
“God is altogether outside the border of time.”[cite: 1]
Stump and Kretzmann
The classical account, following Boethius and Aquinas, treats God's knowledge as timeless rather than as prediction[cite: 1].

Key issue: if God sees the whole of time at once, does divine knowledge determine what humans do, or can timeless knowledge be compatible with libertarian free will?[cite: 1]

Everlasting vs eternal
Everlasting
God is within time and exists at every moment[cite: 1]. God's knowledge of future events is normally described as foreknowledge[cite: 1].
Eternal
God is outside time[cite: 1]. God does not predict future choices because all times are present to God simultaneously[cite: 1].

The distinction matters because it changes how the problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom is understood[cite: 1].

3. Are the attributes of God coherent?

Paradox of the stone — omnipotence

A paradox arises when apparently acceptable premises seem to produce a logically unacceptable or contradictory conclusion[cite: 1].

Question: Can God create a stone so heavy that God cannot lift it?[cite: 1]

If God can create it, there is something God cannot lift[cite: 1]. If God cannot create it, there is something God cannot do[cite: 1]. Either response appears to limit omnipotence[cite: 1].

Averroes / Ibn Rushd
The problem was proposed in this form and later restated by George Mavrodes[cite: 1].
Mavrodes' solution
The task is self-contradictory once omnipotence is assumed[cite: 1]. “Create a stone God cannot lift” combines God's omnipotence with the claim that God cannot lift the stone, so the task is logically impossible[cite: 1].
Savage's solution
The description of the stone is itself incoherent[cite: 1]. An omnipotent being cannot have something it cannot lift, so the proposed object is like a square circle[cite: 1].
Omniscience vs free will

Problem: If God knows beforehand everything humans will do, are their actions still free? Conversely, if human actions are genuinely free, can God be omniscient?[cite: 1]

Stump and Kretzmann — God as eternal
God knows all human actions timelessly, not by making predictions[cite: 1]. If a person freely chooses A, God eternally knows A; if the person had freely chosen B, God would eternally know B[cite: 1]. Divine knowledge is therefore compatible with libertarian free will[cite: 1].
Anthony Kenny — God as everlasting
God's foreknowledge occurs before the human choice, but knowing does not cause the event[cite: 1]. God's knowledge tracks what free creatures will in fact choose without determining their choices[cite: 1].
Swinburne
If future free actions are not yet true or false, God cannot know them without making them non-free[cite: 1]. This creates tension between omniscience and libertarian freedom[cite: 1].
Omnibenevolence vs omnipotence — process theology

If God is both all-powerful and perfectly good, why does God not intervene to prevent suffering? This creates a tension between omnibenevolence and omnipotence[cite: 1].

Process theology
God voluntarily limits divine power and cannot simply control events[cite: 1]. God therefore becomes an empathetic fellow sufferer with creation[cite: 1].

Evaluation question: Does limiting divine power preserve benevolence at the cost of the traditional understanding of omnipotence and divine worship?[cite: 1]

Euthyphro dilemma — omnibenevolence

The dilemma asks: is something morally good because God wills or commands it, or does God will it because it is already good?[cite: 1]

Horn 1 — Kierkegaard
In exceptional cases, God's command can place the individual above ordinary ethical rules[cite: 1]. Ethics is judged in light of God rather than God's authority being judged by ordinary human ethics[cite: 1].
Horn 2 — Plato / Socrates
The pious is loved because it is pious, not made pious merely by being loved[cite: 1]. Goodness therefore does not depend on divine command[cite: 1].
Aquinas — avoids both horns
Natural law bridges divine goodness and human moral reasoning[cite: 1]. God's nature is perfect goodness; eternal law is God's rational ordering of creation; natural law is the part humans can understand[cite: 1]. It is neither an independent standard nor arbitrary divine command[cite: 1].
“The gods love the pious because it is pious.” — Plato, Euthyphro[cite: 1]
